Content Design System

A detailed content design system for CleverTap, consisting of writing guidelines, component rules, and a word list — built to make copy decisions faster, consistent, and scalable across teams and projects.

The Brief

The company had existing voice & tone guidelines, and some scattered rules, but nothing comprehensive enough to govern copy decisions consistently across the product. As the senior UX writer, I led the effort to build a detailed content design system, extending what existed and filling the gaps.

Why It Mattered

Without a system, every copy decision lived in someone's head. A writer on one project wouldn't know what was decided for a similar problem in another. Standards like capitalization required building consensus with PMs and designers. Without documentation, that consensus would evaporate the moment a new project started.

Writing guidelines Core rules that apply across every surface
Writing guidelines

Documenting basic UX writing principles was crucial for training new UX writers and educating other stakeholders about the fundamentals of UX writing and its complexity. Each guideline is numbered, paired with a clear rationale, and includes examples to avoid ambiguity. It covered principles such as heuristics, semantics, numerical formatting, consistency of terminology across flows, and handling recurring edge cases in a complex product.

Design decision
Avoid/Use pairing, not just rules Rules without examples are abstract. Every guideline pairs an avoid case with a use case so writers can pattern-match quickly, not interpret.
Design decision
Numbered for reference Guidelines are numbered so they can be cited in reviews: "this violates rule 08" is faster and less subjective than restating the principle each time.
Component guidelines Copy rules scoped to specific UI patterns
Component guidelines

Component guidelines go one level deeper — rules specific to individual UI patterns, such as confirmation modals, snackbars, empty states, tooltips, etc. The scope and effort required for each component drop significantly when specific guidelines are well defined.

These finer distinctions were developed over time as we figured out the patterns. Some rules were kept on hold and tested over multiple scenarios in different projects to ensure that they were actually scalable before being included in the final document.

Design decision
Works/Avoid structure Each component section shows what good looks like alongside what to avoid — including subtle errors like mismatched button verbs or body copy that asks a question.
Design decision
Format templates included Where a consistent format exists, it's documented as a template: {{Action}} {{Resource}}? for individual actions, {{Action}} {{Resources}}? for bulk. Removes guesswork entirely.
Word list A reference for words that get misused or confused
Word list

The word list addresses a specific problem: the same UI action being named differently across screens because different writers made different calls, or because multiple words could do the same job well.

After carefully considering semantics and heuristics associated with a word, the use cases were clearly documented. Some distinctions were obvious, yet documenting them avoided confusion in the long term and made choices easy to defend for newer writers.

Design decision
Three-column structure Term, use when, don't use when. The "don't use when" column is as important as the "use when" — it's where the boundary between similar terms is drawn precisely.
Design decision
Functional, not aesthetic distinctions Every entry reflects a real product behaviour difference. "View" vs "Load" depends on whether content appears instantly or takes time. The word list makes that logic explicit.
The Outcome
Faster copy decisions
Recurring decisions such as terminology, capitalization, etc. no longer required thinking from scratch or searching through documents to find where they had been made before. Writers could reference the system and move on.
Consistent language
This exercise helped us deliver on the most difficult part of the user experience, i.e., improving consistency. Setting standards and making them accessible and easy to scan made in-product communications more cohesive and consistent.
AI model training
This system also helped train AI models to assist writers in their day-to-day copy work. Hallucinations aside, AI could make better choices as the guidelines improved and became more detailed over time.
